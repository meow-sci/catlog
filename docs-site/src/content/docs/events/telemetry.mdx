This is the passive half of catlog. Twice a second, every live vehicle is sampled;
every 30 seconds of game time, those samples are folded into one summary and the
individual samples are thrown away.

A full window is **60 samples**. Windows do not overlap — a sample at exactly the
boundary closes one window and opens the next.

## Where the vehicle was going

When the game can provide the complete reading, a window also keeps the vehicle's
position **and velocity** at its final sample. Both are measured relative to the
world named by the window, in a frame that does not rotate with the ground.
Position alone would only draw a line between samples; keeping velocity as well
makes it possible for a future viewer to follow the curve between widely spaced
points, including under time warp.

This is the **last** state in the window, not an average. An average position would
not describe anywhere the vehicle really was. It is also all-or-nothing: if any
coordinate is unavailable, or the vehicle changes worlds while the reading is
being taken, the entire state is left out. Catlog never substitutes an origin or
keeps an older world's coordinates under a newer world name.

<Aside type="note" title="Recorded for a future view, not scored">
  No leaderboard reads position or velocity. The state travels with the background summary so it is
  discarded first if the local spool fills; crashes, landings and other gameplay events remain.
</Aside>

## When a window is short

Three things close a window early, and all three produce a summary with fewer than
60 samples:

- **The flight ended.** The partial window is written _before_ the ending, so the
  seconds leading up to a crash are kept rather than discarded.
- **The vehicle vanished** without a proper ending — a docking merge, or repeated
  failed readings.
- **The game shut down.**

Loading a save is different: the partial window is **discarded**, because it would
span two different timelines and its averages would mean nothing.

## Why a missing reading is not zero

`peak_g`, `max_q_pa` and `radar_alt_m` are left out of the summary entirely when
there was nothing to read, rather than being written as zero.

The game only computes structural loads while a vehicle is under full physics. On
rails, or in freefall, there is simply no reading — and the raw value sitting in
memory in that case is zero.

<Aside type="danger" title="Writing zero would break three boards">
  If catlog reported those zeros, **Peak G Survived** and **Max Q Survived** would fill up with
  entries claiming nothing at all, and every real reading would have to compete with noise from
  vehicles that were not being simulated. **Lowest Pass** would be worse still: it is won by the
  smallest number, so a single fabricated zero would take the record and nobody could ever beat it.
  Leaving the field out says "no data", which is what actually happened.
</Aside>

## The two heights, and the one that is not always there

A window carries two altitudes and they answer different questions.

- **Altitude** is measured from the world's average radius. It is always readable,
  because it is a position rather than a physics result, and it is what **Highest
  Altitude** ranks.
- **Height above the ground** is measured from whatever is directly underneath
  you. It is what **Lowest Pass** ranks, and it is the number you would actually
  watch on approach.

The second one is folded over **only the samples that had a ground reading**, so
its minimum, maximum, mean and last do not describe the same set of samples as the
`n` beside them. When no sample in the window had one — a window spent in orbit
has nothing underneath it to measure against — the field is absent rather than
zero, exactly as `peak_g` is.

That is why a flight can hold a Highest Altitude record and no Lowest Pass figure
at all, and why a low run down a canyon and a hover over a mountaintop come out
the opposite way round on the two boards.

## The time-warp reading

Each window also records the highest time-warp speed seen during it; `1` means real
time. Under deep warp a window covers 30 game-seconds with far fewer than 60
samples, so this is what lets a reader tell a fully-sampled window from a sparse
one.

<Aside type="note" title="It is descriptive, and nothing else">
  Time warp is a normal part of playing, and this number disqualifies nothing. It is not a cheat
  signal, it is not scored, and no board reads it. It exists so that "60 samples" and "six samples"
  are distinguishable after the fact.
</Aside>

## Switching it off

This is the largest family of things catlog sends and the most tempting one to turn off, and
you can. It costs you six boards — Peak G Survived, Max Q Survived, Highest
Altitude, Lowest Pass, Fastest Surface Speed and Fastest Orbital Speed — and
nothing else supplies them. It also removes the only thing catlog is allowed to discard on its
own, described just below, so a spool under pressure has nothing left to shed.

See [Turning things off](/catlog/start/turning-things-off/).

## The one thing catlog is willing to lose

Telemetry summaries are the only record catlog will discard. If the local spool
grows past its limit — a long offline stretch, a server outage — the oldest
summaries are dropped, oldest first, and everything that scores is kept.

That is a deliberate ordering: losing sample resolution costs you a slightly
coarser chart; losing a RUD costs you a leaderboard row.
